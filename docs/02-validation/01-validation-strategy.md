# Validation Strategy

Validation exists to decide whether LOGOS Engine has enough evidence to move from Foundation into deeper Product, Engineering, and Go-to-market commitments without confusing conviction for proof.

The strategy is not to validate "the idea" in the abstract. The strategy is to test whether the project is solving the right problem for the right audience, whether the proposed approach creates observable clarity, whether the local-first structured-documentation model is acceptable, and whether the project can advance without violating its principles or boundaries.

## Validation Objective

The Validation phase should reduce the most dangerous uncertainties before the project commits to product specification, engineering architecture, market positioning, or monetization.

The primary validation objective is:

> Determine whether people who are starting meaningful projects experience enough ambiguity, decision drift, and documentation pain that a local-first, AI-assisted, decision-oriented documentation engine would be useful before serious execution begins.

This objective tests the following Foundation claims first:

- People often begin projects before foundational decisions are explicit enough to guide execution.
- The most important artifact is an explicit, traceable decision, not a polished document.
- Structured documentation is valuable when it exposes decisions, assumptions, gaps, risks, and next actions.
- The initial audience values local, inspectable, Git-friendly, text-based project knowledge.
- AI can help clarify intent when constrained by structure, traceability, and user confirmation.
- App-based business creation is an origin case and proving ground, but the broader need applies to other meaningful project types.

The phase should make these decisions possible:

- Continue with the current thesis.
- Narrow the audience or first profile.
- Revise the problem definition.
- Adjust the local-first or AI-led workflow assumptions.
- Defer or reject product directions that create false success, over-automation, or scope drift.
- Stop or reframe if users do not experience the problem strongly enough.

The first confidence target is not market scale, revenue, or broad adoption. It is evidence that the core workflow produces clarity users recognize as meaningfully better than their starting notes, generic AI chat, or ad hoc documentation.

## Validation Scope

### In Scope

This phase validates the conditions required before committing to product, engineering, or go-to-market direction:

- **Problem reality:** whether users actually experience project-start ambiguity, hidden assumptions, inconsistent documentation, or decision drift.
- **Audience fit:** which audience segment feels the problem most strongly and can adopt a local documentation workflow.
- **Current alternatives:** how users currently cope through notes, templates, generic AI chat, project management tools, workshops, or immediate building.
- **Behavioral value:** whether structured questioning and decision capture change what users understand, decide, or document.
- **Workflow acceptability:** whether users tolerate AI-led intake, review of proposed decisions, marked uncertainty, and local file-based outputs.
- **Trust model:** whether users value inspectability, local-first storage, explicit provider configuration, and reviewable AI output.
- **Generality boundary:** whether the broader "structured documentation before execution" framing holds beyond the app-based business origin case.
- **Minimum validation threshold:** whether at least one real user can complete an end-to-end local documentation flow and produce a foundation package judged clearer and more useful than the starting state.

### Deferred

These topics are important but should not lead the first validation sequence:

- **Willingness to pay:** deferred until problem severity, audience fit, and workflow value are clearer.
- **Hosted collaboration demand:** deferred because cloud collaboration may violate or reshape Local First.
- **Broad profile coverage:** deferred until one or a small number of profiles prove the decision/document model.
- **Agent-pack implementation outcomes:** deferred until canonical documents are reliably useful.
- **Marketplace or plugin demand:** deferred until profile quality and governance are validated.
- **Large-scale quantitative adoption:** deferred until the product has a stable enough workflow to measure.

### Out of Scope

This strategy does not validate:

- Final product specification.
- Detailed UX/UI.
- Technical architecture.
- Database schema.
- Launch campaign execution.
- Sales process.
- Legal or compliance claims.
- Market-size claims.
- Full economic model or pricing package.

Those topics belong in downstream Validation, Product, Engineering, Go-to-market, Operations, or Legal/Compliance artifacts.

### Risk of Deferral

Deferred items become risky if they are used to justify irreversible commitments. For example, pricing should not drive product strategy before problem severity is understood; hosted collaboration should not drive architecture before local value is proven; broad profile expansion should not happen before the core structured-documentation loop works.

## Validation Priorities

Validation should be prioritized by risk, dependency, reversibility, and cost of being wrong.

### Priority 1: Problem and Audience Before Solution

The first priority is validating that the problem exists independently of LOGOS Engine.

Key claims:

- Users start meaningful projects with ambiguous intent.
- This ambiguity creates real pain: rework, inconsistent documentation, weak scoping, hidden assumptions, or late discovery of constraints.
- The pain is strong enough for users to accept a clarification workflow before execution.
- The strongest early audience can be identified behaviorally, not only by title.

Reason: if the problem is weak or the audience is wrong, solution validation is misleading.

### Priority 2: Current Alternatives and Dissatisfaction

The second priority is understanding what users already do and where current alternatives fail.

Key claims:

- Freeform notes, templates, generic AI chat, immediate building, and project management tools solve parts of the problem but leave structural gaps.
- Users can explain why those alternatives are insufficient for pre-execution clarity.
- The project is not merely duplicating a workflow users already find good enough.

Reason: the project must prove it is better than existing coping behavior, not just conceptually cleaner.

### Priority 3: Structured Decision Value

The third priority is validating the central mechanism: decisions before documents.

Key claims:

- Users gain useful clarity when decisions, assumptions, risks, gaps, and open questions are made explicit.
- Users can review proposed decisions and distinguish them from confirmed decisions.
- Generated documents are more useful when tied to structured state than when written as generic AI prose.

Reason: this is the core thesis. If structured decisions do not improve documentation usefulness, the project should be revised.

### Priority 4: Workflow and Trust Fit

The fourth priority is validating whether the intended workflow matches the audience's constraints.

Key claims:

- AI-led intake feels better than a deterministic questionnaire while still producing structured state.
- Local-first, Git-friendly outputs are valued by the intended audience.
- Users tolerate visible uncertainty and confirmation steps because they produce trust.
- Users understand and value the difference between canonical documents, structured state, derived artifacts, and AI output.

Reason: even a real problem can fail if the workflow is too heavy, too technical, or not trusted.

### Priority 5: Generality Beyond the Origin Case

The fifth priority is validating whether the project should remain broadly framed across project types.

Key claims:

- The app-based business origin case is not the only meaningful context.
- Other project types also suffer from ambiguous intent and weak documentation before execution.
- The profile model can support multiple outcomes without collapsing into generic templates.

Reason: broad ambition is important, but premature generality can dilute validation.

### Priority 6: Economics and Growth

Economic model, business model, pricing, distribution, and growth validation should follow only after the problem, audience, mechanism, and workflow are sufficiently supported.

Reason: willingness to pay and go-to-market evidence are not useful if the core clarity workflow is not yet valuable.

## Evidence Standards

Evidence standards should match the claim type. This phase should avoid false precision, invented metrics, and evidence theater.

### Problem Evidence

Strong evidence:

- Users describe the problem in their own words before seeing the solution.
- Users can provide recent concrete examples of project ambiguity, rework, inconsistent documentation, or late-discovered constraints.
- Users currently use workarounds that show the pain is real.
- Users can identify consequences of not solving the problem.

Weak evidence:

- Users agree abstractly that "documentation is important."
- Users praise the concept after hearing the pitch.
- Users say they would use it without describing real behavior or pain.

Invalidating evidence:

- Intended users do not recognize the problem.
- They experience the problem but do not care enough to change behavior.
- They already have low-friction alternatives that solve the problem well enough.

### Audience Evidence

Strong evidence:

- A specific audience segment repeatedly shows the same pain and context of use.
- Users have the capabilities assumed in the Audience Definition or can adopt the workflow with little friction.
- Users value local, inspectable, text-based documentation.

Weak evidence:

- Broad interest across many personas without clear intensity.
- Positive reactions from people outside the initial workflow context.
- Demographic labels without behavioral evidence.

Invalidating evidence:

- The intended audience rejects the local-first or structured-documentation workflow.
- The strongest demand comes from an audience requiring a different product boundary, such as cloud collaboration or no local setup.

### Behavior and Workflow Evidence

Strong evidence:

- Users complete a realistic intake flow using their own project.
- They identify missing decisions or assumptions they had not made explicit.
- They revise, confirm, or reject proposed decisions thoughtfully.
- They use the generated documentation to guide a real next step.

Weak evidence:

- Users skim a demo and say it looks useful.
- Users generate documents but do not review or act on them.
- Users prefer the output because it is polished rather than because it clarifies decisions.

Invalidating evidence:

- The workflow creates more friction than clarity.
- Users treat AI output as final because the system fails to signal uncertainty.
- Users prefer generic AI chat or manual notes after trying the structured workflow.

### Trust and Boundary Evidence

Strong evidence:

- Users notice and value inspectability, local files, explicit provider configuration, and traceability.
- Users can explain what is confirmed, assumed, proposed, and incomplete.
- Users are willing to proceed because uncertainty is visible.

Weak evidence:

- Users say privacy matters but do not inspect or use local controls.
- Users trust output only because it sounds confident.

Invalidating evidence:

- Users cannot understand where project knowledge lives.
- Remote AI usage or generated content creates distrust.
- The system requires boundary violations to feel useful.

### Economic and Business Evidence

Economic evidence is suggestive only until problem and workflow evidence exist.

Strong evidence later may include:

- Users have budget or existing spending for project clarification, consulting, tooling, or documentation workflows.
- Users can name avoided costs or saved time from reduced ambiguity.
- Users return to the workflow across project stages or multiple projects.

Weak evidence:

- Users say they might pay without a concrete context.
- Pricing is inferred from competitor categories before the category is validated.

Invalidating evidence:

- Users value the output but not enough to allocate time, money, workflow change, or trust.

## Validation Sequence

The sequence should protect the project from validating downstream features before upstream claims.

### Step 1: Foundation Review

Purpose: confirm that thesis, problem, audience, principles, boundaries, success definition, and glossary are internally consistent enough to validate.

Outputs:

- Initial assumptions list.
- Initial risk map.
- Candidate hypotheses.
- Known evidence gaps.

Feeds:

- Core Assumptions.
- Hypotheses.

### Step 2: Assumption Mapping

Purpose: convert Foundation claims into explicit assumptions grouped by thesis, problem, audience, behavior, workflow, trust, economics, and generality.

Outputs:

- Critical assumptions.
- Secondary assumptions.
- Killing assumptions.
- Dependency relationships.

Feeds:

- Hypotheses.
- Research Plan.
- Experiments.

### Step 3: Problem and Audience Research

Purpose: test whether the pain exists, who feels it most strongly, and how they currently cope.

Methods may include interviews, observation of existing project notes, review of current documentation workflows, or analysis of real scoping situations.

Outputs:

- Evidence about problem severity, frequency, urgency, current alternatives, and audience fit.
- Refined audience segments.
- Initial invalidation signals if the problem is weak.

Feeds:

- Evidence Log.
- Decision Record.
- Hypotheses.

### Step 4: Workflow Prototype Validation

Purpose: test whether AI-led intake, decision proposals, marked assumptions, diagnostics, and canonical Markdown outputs create observable clarity for a real project.

Methods may include a lightweight guided session, manual concierge flow, prototype run, or mocked local workflow. The method should be chosen in Experiments, not finalized here.

Outputs:

- Before-and-after documentation.
- User review of missing decisions and clarified assumptions.
- Evidence of workflow friction or trust.
- Signals about local-first and Git-friendly value.

Feeds:

- Experiments.
- Evidence Log.
- Decision Record.
- Validation Report.

### Step 5: Boundary and Trust Review

Purpose: confirm that the product can be useful without violating its boundaries.

Topics:

- No false authority.
- No covert data movement.
- User-owned decisions.
- Derived artifacts remain derived.
- Local-first source of truth.

Outputs:

- Boundary risks.
- Required safeguards.
- Stop or revise conditions if usefulness depends on boundary violations.

Feeds:

- Decision Record.
- Validation Report.
- Product Scope.

### Step 6: Economic and Business Direction

Purpose: only after user value is supported, examine whether there is a plausible path to sustainability.

Outputs:

- Economic assumptions.
- Business model options.
- Pricing and adoption questions.
- Distribution risks.

Feeds:

- Economic Model.
- Business Model.
- Go-to-market planning later.

### Parallelizable Work

Can run in parallel after Foundation Review:

- Assumption extraction.
- Existing evidence collection.
- Interview candidate sourcing.
- Review of current alternatives.
- Lightweight prototype planning.

Must wait for upstream evidence:

- Pricing validation should wait for problem and workflow evidence.
- Go-to-market strategy should wait for audience fit.
- Engineering commitment should wait for workflow and boundary validation.
- Profile expansion should wait for proof that the core documentation model works.

## Invalidating Conditions

Invalidating conditions should be defined before detailed experiments so the project does not advance only because effort was already invested.

### Stop Conditions

Stop or substantially reframe if:

- Intended users do not recognize the problem as real.
- Users experience the problem but do not see value in structured clarification before execution.
- Users consistently produce clearer documentation with simpler manual workflows.
- The system only becomes useful when it violates core boundaries: remote-first state, autonomous decisions, hidden AI authority, or polished output over structure.
- AI-led intake cannot avoid false confidence without becoming unusable.

### Pivot Conditions

Pivot if:

- The strongest user pain belongs to a narrower audience than currently defined.
- The workflow is valuable only for consultants, agencies, or a specific project type.
- Users value diagnostics and gap review more than full document generation.
- Users value local structured state but not AI-led conversation.
- Users value the outcome but need a different interface than CLI/TUI.

### Revision Conditions

Revise Foundation or Validation artifacts if:

- App-based business evidence does not generalize to other project types.
- The target audience definition is too broad to guide product decisions.
- Evidence shows that the main problem is not documentation quality but another upstream issue.
- Users misunderstand key terms such as decision, assumption, validation, or canonical source.
- Success criteria reward output volume rather than clarity.

### Boundary Violation Conditions

Do not advance a direction if:

- It requires unreviewed AI-generated decisions.
- It depends on undisclosed remote provider transmission.
- It treats generated presentation artifacts as canonical.
- It pressures users into fake certainty.
- It turns LOGOS Engine into a generic project management suite or app generator.

## Decision Gates

### Gate 1: Foundation Readiness

**Purpose:** decide whether the Foundation documents are coherent enough to validate.

**Required evidence:**

- Drafted Foundation documents.
- Explicit success definition.
- Known boundaries and terms.
- Initial list of assumptions and risks.

**Decision criteria:**

- No unresolved contradiction blocks validation.
- The central thesis, problem, audience, and success definition are clear enough to produce assumptions.
- App-based business is framed as origin/proving ground, not hard boundary.

**Possible decisions:**

- Go to assumption mapping.
- Revise Foundation.
- Defer unclear terms or boundaries with explicit notes.

**Decision owner:** founder/user, supported by reviewer or agent review.

**Downstream effect:** allows Core Assumptions and Hypotheses to proceed.

### Gate 2: Problem and Audience Evidence

**Purpose:** decide whether the project is solving a real problem for a plausible early audience.

**Required evidence:**

- Qualitative evidence from real or representative users.
- Current alternatives and coping behavior.
- Examples of ambiguity, rework, missing decisions, or documentation failure.
- Evidence of audience capability and workflow context.

**Decision criteria:**

- Users describe the pain before being sold the solution.
- The pain is severe or frequent enough to justify behavior change.
- A specific early audience can be prioritized.

**Possible decisions:**

- Go to workflow validation.
- Narrow the audience.
- Revise the problem.
- Stop if the pain is not meaningful.

**Decision owner:** founder/user, with evidence recorded in Evidence Log and rationale in Decision Record.

**Downstream effect:** blocks Product and Engineering commitments if unmet.

### Gate 3: Structured Decision Workflow

**Purpose:** decide whether the core mechanism creates clarity.

**Required evidence:**

- Before-and-after documentation from a real project.
- Evidence that at least one missing decision, risky assumption, contradiction, or next step became explicit.
- User review of proposed decisions and generated documents.
- Diagnostics or validation output that is actionable.

**Decision criteria:**

- Documentation is clearer, more specific, and more useful than the starting state.
- The user can distinguish confirmed, proposed, assumed, unknown, and generated content.
- The workflow produces more clarity than generic AI chat or manual notes for the same task.

**Possible decisions:**

- Go to prototype/product shaping.
- Revise workflow.
- Reduce scope.
- Stop or reframe if structured decision capture is not valuable.

**Decision owner:** founder/user, with reviewer assessment where possible.

**Downstream effect:** informs Product, Engineering, and Experiment design.

### Gate 4: Trust and Boundary Fit

**Purpose:** decide whether usefulness can be achieved without violating principles.

**Required evidence:**

- User reaction to local-first storage, provider disclosure, confirmation steps, and marked uncertainty.
- Review of whether generated outputs remain derived from canonical source.
- Evidence that safety and explicitness do not make the workflow unusable.

**Decision criteria:**

- Users trust the workflow more because uncertainty and provenance are visible.
- The system does not need hidden AI authority or remote-first behavior to feel valuable.
- Boundary safeguards are acceptable to the target audience.

**Possible decisions:**

- Proceed with current trust model.
- Revise safeguards or interface.
- Defer remote or collaboration features.
- Stop any direction that requires boundary violations.

**Decision owner:** founder/user; boundary-sensitive decisions should be explicitly recorded.

**Downstream effect:** constrains Product, Engineering, Operations, and AI behavior.

### Gate 5: Validation Phase Recommendation

**Purpose:** decide whether to advance, revise, pivot, or stop after initial validation work.

**Required evidence:**

- Evidence Log.
- Decision Record.
- Validation findings from problem, audience, workflow, and boundary tests.
- Explicit unresolved assumptions.
- Clear statement of what remains unvalidated.

**Decision criteria:**

- Enough evidence exists to justify one of the phase completion decisions.
- The decision does not rely on vanity metrics or false success.
- Remaining assumptions are explicit and acceptable for the next phase.

**Possible decisions:**

- Advance to Product.
- Revise Foundation or Validation assumptions.
- Pivot audience, profile, workflow, or scope.
- Stop or archive.

**Decision owner:** founder/user.

**Downstream effect:** feeds Validation Report and determines whether Product, Engineering, or Go-to-market work should proceed.

## Open Questions

- Which project type should be used first for real-user validation: app-based business, broader software project, client scoping, creator project, or internal initiative?
- How many validation sessions are enough before narrowing the initial audience?
- What reviewer rubric should be used to compare starting notes against generated documentation?
- How should the project measure "clarity gained" without creating vanity metrics?
- What evidence would justify validating pricing before the workflow is fully proven?
