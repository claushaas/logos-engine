# Go-to-market

Go-to-market defines how the product reaches the right market, communicates its value, acquires users or customers, converts demand, launches publicly, and measures commercial traction.

It translates Product and Validation into a market-facing strategy: category, positioning, message, segments, channels, launch, funnel, sales motion, packaging, assets, growth metrics, and market risks.

This phase must not redefine the product specification, technical implementation, or operational support model. Its job is to make market entry explicit before public execution begins.

## Central Question

How will this product reach the right market with a clear message, viable channels, measurable conversion, and a credible path to traction?

## Phase Output

By the end of Go-to-market, the project should have:

- a go-to-market brief;
- market definition;
- positioning;
- messaging;
- customer segment prioritization;
- channel strategy;
- launch plan;
- conversion funnel;
- sales strategy;
- pricing and packaging strategy;
- marketing asset inventory;
- growth metrics;
- go-to-market risk register.

## Out of Scope

Go-to-market should not contain:

- founding thesis;
- core product scope decisions;
- detailed UX specification;
- UI component specification;
- system architecture;
- API contracts;
- deployment strategy;
- customer support process;
- incident management process;
- financial operations process.

Those belong to Foundation, Product, Engineering, or Operations.

## Boundary with Validation

Validation determines whether the project deserves to advance and whether the business logic is plausible.

Go-to-market determines how that validated logic becomes a market-facing motion.

Examples:

| Validation | Go-to-market |
|---|---|
| Economic viability | Pricing and packaging |
| Business model logic | Revenue motion and sales strategy |
| Evidence from research and experiments | Market message and channel assumptions |
| Core assumptions | GTM risks and experiments |
| Validation decision | Launch readiness |

## Boundary with Product

Product defines what should be built and how it should be experienced.

Go-to-market defines how that product is understood, discovered, evaluated, purchased, and adopted.

Examples:

| Product | Go-to-market |
|---|---|
| Product promise | Positioning and messaging |
| Target user | Customer segments and buyer roles |
| Product scope | Launch offer and packaging |
| UX and feature set | Funnel and activation logic |
| Acceptance criteria | Launch readiness criteria |

## Boundary with Operations

Go-to-market defines the market-entry motion.

Operations defines how the product, customers, support, reporting, revenue, risks, and continuous improvement are managed after launch.

Examples:

| Go-to-market | Operations |
|---|---|
| Launch plan | Release coordination and operational cadence |
| Conversion funnel | Customer success and retention process |
| Sales strategy | Customer handoff and account operations |
| Pricing and packaging | Billing operations and failed payment handling |
| Growth metrics | Operational metrics and reporting cadence |

## Completion Standard

Go-to-market is complete when the product can be presented, launched, distributed, sold or adopted, measured, and adjusted without relying on implicit market assumptions.

If the team must guess the category, message, segment, channel, funnel, launch motion, pricing presentation, sales approach, required assets, or growth signals, Go-to-market is still too vague.
